Book Recommendations

My top 11 healthcare reads

Reading List · June 2026

Over the past year, as an avid reader by heart I have delved into over 50 books relating to American healthcare. I wish I could pull up on my library records all of their titles to deliver a proper book recommendation post, but I am only able to go back to this April. The books on this list define recent reads that have had a profound impact on how I think about healthcare and biomedical ethics.

Here’s the list of my top 11 (you’ll recognize some from my blog posts):

  • A Giant Leap by Robert Wachter
    Explores transformative concepts and significant advancements within the fields of healthcare and biomedical ethics.
  • Being Mortal by Atul Gawande
    Examines aging and the end of life, arguing that modern medicine must prioritize the quality of life and patient well-being over merely prolonging survival.
  • Riding the Bus with My Sister: A True Life Journey by Rachel Simon
    Chronicles the author's journey of reconnecting with her sister, who has an intellectual disability and spends her days riding city buses.
  • Disability Visibility (Adapted for Young Adults): 17 First-Person Stories for Today by Alice Wong
  • Compassionomics by Stephen Trzeciak and Anthony Mazzarelli
    Reviews scientific evidence demonstrating that compassionate healthcare improves physical outcomes for patients, lowers medical costs, and reduces physician burnout.
  • A Disability History of the United States by Kim E. Nielsen
  • Grief and Loss by Katherine Walsh
    A text detailing the emotional, clinical, and ethical dimensions of navigating death, bereavement, and the grieving process.
  • “If You Knew the Conditions”: A Chronicle of the Indian Medical Service and American Indian Health Care, 1908-1955 by David H. Dejong
    Chronicles the history of the Indian Medical Service between 1908 and 1955, exploring the systemic underfunding and assimilation-driven health policies faced by Native American communities.
  • The Spirit Catches You and You Fall Down by Anne Fadiman
    An ethnographic account detailing the tragic cultural miscommunications between a Hmong refugee family and the American medical establishment over the treatment of their daughter's severe epilepsy.
  • Migration and Health, Edited by Sandro Galea and Catherine K. Ettman
    Examines the complex systemic health challenges, vulnerabilities, and healthcare access issues faced by migrating and displaced populations.
  • The Invisible Kingdom by Meghan O’ Rourke
    Explores the often-overlooked struggles of patients living with chronic, difficult-to-diagnose, and "invisible" illnesses, and how the healthcare system often fails them.

"If You Knew the Conditions"

The evolution of Native American health care

History & Policy · June 2026 · 4 min read

A Chronicle of the Indian Medical Service

“Take as many whites as there are Indians and give them as much sickness as the Indian has been having, and the whites would soon be poverty stricken, ambitionless, and immoral. The sicker the Indian gets the worse he lives. Health is the basis of all life.”

— Dr. C. A. Harper, Health Officer for the State of Wisconsin, 1933

The progressive-minded reformer did not mince words, nor did the President of the American Indian Defense Association and Director of the Institute of Public Health at Columbia University, calling the Indian Service “the most disgraceful apology of scientific or humane medical care” in the country. This was in spite of the provisions specifically related to the United States providing Indians health care in 31 out of 389 ratified Indian treaties.

The Indian medical service’s primary flaws were in its perpetual state of severe under-funding, and the very morals guiding the provision of health care to the Native American community. The curative and crisis-oriented service was oriented to the mission of aiding Indians to complete cultural assimilation– their “only means to salvation”.

For decades, the Indian Service was compelled to be a sub-par organization that did too little to address the various public health challenges Native Americans faced: trachoma, tuberculosis, high material and infant mortality rates, poor sanitation measures, and inadequate and low quality staffing. The nature of Indian reservations compelled them to be distanced from populated areas and often in isolated, roadless areas. For instance, “In 1944, for example, the physician vacancy rate exceeded 30%, with 100 vacancies out of 321 positions. One position on the Rosebud Sioux Reservation was vacant for six years before being filled”.

This was primarily due to physician salaries being about half of that in private practice during more well-funded years, the high physician load (being on-call 24/7, 11 mos/yr), and poor working and living conditions. For example, “One agency physician lived with his wife in a ten by ten room within the hospital” and even the commissioner of Indian affairs, Collier, argued that physicians and nurses worked “under frontier conditions”.

“By the 1920s, the status of Indian health was at least two generations behind the national average”. By 1935, enormous progress had been made under Collier’s leadership; however, “Indian health status remained two decades behind that of the general population”. The Meriam Report, a 872-page report done by a think tank, the Institute for Government Research, revealed startling health disparities for Native Americans: “Infant and early childhood mortality rates twice the national average and a tuberculosis death rate seven times the national average (with Indians in Arizona experiencing a death rate seventeen times higher)”. Moreover, “In 1954, American Indians faced a measles death rate twenty times the non-Indian rate. Deaths from pneumonia and influenza were four times the non-Indian rate and infant deaths were three times the comparable non-Indian rate”.

Particularly, there were three populations that suffered the greatest under subpar health care quality and access: children, the disabled, and Alaska Natives. As Native American children were forcibly ripped away from their communities to attend boarding schools seeking to assimilate them into Western society, they were greeted with overcrowded facilities with poor environmental conditions that caused significantly higher disease rates than in Indians staying at reserves. Despite 25 years of criticism from the Indian Service, boarding school officials remained disturbingly indifferent. Additionally, the lack of pediatricians and nutritionists in the service only contributed to the principal causes of death: infant and childhood diseases. Furthermore, the Papago Reservation Superintendent reported the tribe’s population which resembled that of medieval Europe: “Of approximately 260 infants born each year, one-fourth die within twelve months; at the age of 6 there are only 160 left; at the age of eighteen only 125.”

As for the disabled Native American community, they had their own disturbing history with the federal government: “When Congress enacted into law the Social Security Act of 1935, it required states to provide services for all crippled children, including American Indian and Alaska Native, or face the loss of federal grants-in-aid. Arizona and New Mexico elected to forfeit federal aid rather than provide services for Indian children, basing their decisions on the Indians’ ward (and non-tax-paying) status and their state organic acts, which denied Indian wards services from the state… By midcentury, for example, 145 counties provided services for crippled children. Yet, this covered just 60% of the Indian population. Of this number, only one- third of American Indians had access to such treatment. In order to obtain services, it was common for children to travel 100 miles or more.”

As for Alaska Natives, they were let down by the United States by not following their purchase treaty provisions with Russia. The 1867 treaty that states that the U.S. would provide the same care and protection the rest of the nation enjoyed to Alaska Natives was not followed through. The Alaska Native Service was created in 1914, and two years later the first non-school hospital was opened– 49 years after the treaty was signed.

Considering early U.S. attempts at providing medical care to Native Americans was purely to prevent nearby military post soldiers from contracting infectious diseases such as smallpox, the federal government’s half-hearted attempt at providing Indians healthcare until the 20th century looks comparatively not so bad. But, the bar is *extremely *low. A big part of that stems from how American Indians were not all formally considered citizens until The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924. Another concerning fact is that Indian Service funds were often congressionally allocated “Based on the moral principle of charity rather than on any legal obligation”-- troubling, considering the subjective nature of morality, and the ignorance of federal treaties it rested on.

Facing a post-WWII wave of nationalism and conservatism, the decades-long desire of Congress to squash federal funding towards Native American health was realized. Through the Indian Health Transfer Act of 1954 local and state health authorities were called to assume greater responsibility for Indian health as the Indian Service migrated into the Public Health Service, a better funded and staffed federal agency.

Present-day tribes are faced with the freedom and responsibility that comes with partially being responsible for ensuring their tribe’s access to health care. They are able to combine Western and traditional medicine approaches in a way that respects their culture, values, and religion with healthcare innovation. The Indian Health Service still provides care that nearly 25% of Native Americans rely on, and Medicare, Medicaid, and the Children’s Health Insurance Program contribute as well. However, tribes have turned to gaming operations to help fund the health services of their people as well.

In October 2024, Arizona, California, New Mexico, and Oregon were given waivers that allowed state Medicaid agencies to cover traditional medicine practices. These waivers and the terms of approval were developed with Tribal leadership partnerships and advisory groups with the aim to financially support tribes in providing culturally-appropriate health care. Overall, there is still work to be done in reducing health inequities between Native Americans and that of typical Americans. Underfunding and racist ideology and discrimination centrally guided how the U.S. government provided, or lack thereof, of health care to Native communities since the nineteenth century, but, luckily, we’ve come a long way.

Quote Spotlight

Inspiration · June 2026

“Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic.”

— Arthur C. Clarke

Clarke’s third law, a mention to the British science fiction writer and futurist, describes a timeless ideal relevant to technological innovation’s power and influence to shape humanity. This quote first appeared in the footnote of his 1973 revision of *Profiles of the Future*, and is both inspiring and also revealing of the nature of technology. Artificial intelligence stands out as a technology that we are still beginning to understand and develop and I am excited to see how healthcare technology continues to innovate and save lives– all while intentional regulations, laws, and the Coalition for Health AI (CHAI) work to ensure safe steps forward.

The State of Medical Education

Education · June 2026

“In 1950, medical information doubled every fifty years. Today, it’s every couple of months.”

— A Giant Leap, pg. 200

It simply is inefficient and unsustainable to expect medical schools’ curriculum and medical students themselves humanly keep up with all this knowledge.

There have been recent efforts to change medical training: introducing medical students to patients early in their first year, and a focus on working in interdisciplinary teams, improving care systems, and addressing health disparities that have been widely implemented (201). There are also fewer lectures and more case-based discussions (201). Additionally, medical school students and residents are increasingly encouraged to use AI tools responsibly– factoring in prompt engineering, how to spot hallucination and bias, and managing ethics and compliance issues (208).

Anyone intending to be a physician can also celebrate Marc Triola’s (professor and director of NYU’s Institute for Innovations in Medical Education) perspective on what should be taught less of: “Rote memorization and fact regurgitation are just going to be less important” (202-203; 208).

Funnily enough, there’s only one set of facts that Dr. Watcher, author of *A Giant Leap* and chair of the department of medicine at the University of California, San Francisco, found faculty could agree on, after “Countless hours of debate on curricular purging”: no longer requiring the memorization of the Krebs cycle. He remarks that “This consensus likely stemmed more from physicians’ collective trauma over learning this biochemical energy pathway in med school than from any rigorous assessment of its educational merit” (208).

So, where does the future of medical school and residency training program education go from here?

We’ll just have to wait and see.

Stifled Innovation

Industry · June 2026

The end of my blog post about A Giant Leap made a pointed remark at Epic, the largest electronic health system in the United States:

“Healthcare’s primary function is to maximize human health, and if wise AI implementation can assist with that, then let it be– Epic’s monopolistic and anticompetitive practices be damned (Watcher 214-222).”

Let’s double click on that.

The origins of Faulkner’s company began in 1979 and it has grown to service over 250 million Americans and over half of U.S. hospital beds (214-215). Epic has rolled out over a 100 AI initiatives that can be built in and purchased with their EHR (216). There is one problem though: their AI tools are, in many cases, not as good as those of other companies. This leaves healthcare systems with the decision to stick with Epic’s offerings or to purchase integratable AI tools for their EHR system (217).

In speaking to several health system technology leaders, Dr. Watcher observed an interesting theme: all leaders insisted to remain anonymous when talking about Epic (219). They went further, saying that for them to invest in third-party AI tools they would have to be "extraordinarily better” than Epic’s (219).

It goes beyond the convenience of tool integration or cost savings sticking with Epic provides– rather, it’s about not having to deal with keeping up with 50 vendors a week coming to them, and having to constantly keep up with the constant healthcare AI innovations (219). Sticking with Epic is the safe choice, especially as Epic introduced a single pricing option to access their entire AI portfolio (219).

In response to Epic’s recent lawsuits alleging they engage in monopolistic, anticompetitive practices, past president of Physicians for a National Health Program Adam Gaffney states, “‘Handing the keys over to a private monopoly doesn’t make sense. You need to think of a national medical record system as a public service’” (221).

As Oracle is now the EHR of choice for Veteran Administration hospitals, where 70% of medical trainees rotate through, and Epic continues to be the EHR of choice for the majority of academic hospitals, the positive experiences of future physicians could have a say in if Oracle obtains more healthcare system customers. But, it is quite unlikely given the enormous amount of control Epic, or rather Faulkner, the sole owner has over the EHR and EHR AI tools space.

Interdisciplinary Studies: Healthcare Technology Law & Regulation

University of Georgia · Franklin College of Arts and Sciences · A.B. Interdisciplinary Studies

Designed for students at the intersection of public health, technology law, and public policy, this major investigates the regulatory frameworks and ethical foundations required to protect society's most vulnerable. Requiring 24 hours of major coursework, the curriculum examines how marginalized groups such as the sick, disabled, elderly, immigrants, and those suffering from mental health and substance abuse issues interact with the healthcare system. It provides specialized training in healthcare management, American law, and how ethics interplay with law, business, and medicine.

All students must complete these courses:

  • HPAM 3600: Introduction to Health Policy
  • One course in Artificial Intelligence (e.g., ARTI 2130, ARTI 2550, MIST 5400)
  • INTS 4990R: Undergraduate Research Thesis or Final Project
