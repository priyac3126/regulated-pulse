“Equity is the Only Acceptable Goal”

The Enduring Legacy of Paul Farmer

Book Insights · November 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Published 2009.

Reading Mountains Beyond Mountains as someone with limited knowledge about global health was eye-opening and I am beyond inspired by what Paul Farmer and the rest of the Partners in Health team was able to accomplish across the world in increasing access to healthcare in remote, poverty-stricken areas of the world.

I could not recommend reading this book more highly– it has been life-changing and has made me interested in pursuing a career in global health– an area I thought I had crossed off years ago to focus on creating change in the United States, my home country. But, who’s to say you can’t try pursuing both, simultaneously? That’s what Paul Farmer did, as he was a Harvard and Brigham and Women’s Hospital Professor as he led international projects in places such as Haiti, a country he served since his gap years from medical school. Even while in medical school, he remained devoted to building up the nation’s access to care, often only showing up to Harvard Medical School for lab practicums or exams (Kidder 84).

Farmer had an unconventional approach to medicine, one that involved even making seven hour treks out to two patients’ homes when they missed their follow-up appointments. He did this even in the 2000s when he had achieved great international acclaim and those close to him would say, in light of his insanely busy schedule, that it may not be the best use of his time. To leave it to the community health workers Partners in Health, his nonprofit, employed.

Farmer was extremely charitable throughout his life and had a strong moral compass– one that did not falter even when public health organizations such as WHO argued as cost-effectiveness being a barrier to treating multidrug-resistant tuberculosis when those dollars would go farther in treating typical TB. His salary from Harvard and Brigham, along with his other earnings would go directly to the bookkeeper at PIH to continue the good work the organization was doing– and there was never enough money to go around for that. Even till this day, because of the nature of the problems PIH is tackling (healthcare inequality worldwide), the nonprofit can always use more resources to expand their services.

Jim Yong Kim, another co-founder of Partners in Health was also instrumental in reducing the prices of second-line tuberculosis drugs, an integral step in expanding international efforts to combating MDR in underprivileged areas. For example, by 2000, the Green Light Committee, a committee he helped create to control the distribution of second-line TB drugs, paid around 95% less for four of the second-line drugs than they would have in 1996, and 86% less for two others (Kidder 173). This meant to treat one case of four-drug-resistant MDR it went from costing $15,000 to merely $1,500 (Kidder 173). This difference is the difference between an organization like the World Health Organization believing it is cost-effective and practical to treat thousands afflicted with this kind of tuberculosis. PIH fought for MDR treatment inclusion in WHO TB programs because it saw that as tuberculosis afflicted more and more people that did not have consistent, high-quality access to healthcare, their bodies would develop immunities to many first-line TB drugs. PIH saw this in Lima, Peru, a community they served, where some patients even came to them with a form of TB resistant to every known drug (Kidder 172). PIH recognized that unless a large, successful international MDR program was established, the disease would only continue to afflict more despite the tools to control the suffering (Kidder 181).

As of 2000, two million people still died of tuberculosis a year so it was imperative that the global health community did what they could to limit the disease's toll on the world (Kidder 181).

I urge everyone to read this book, not just to learn more about global health or tuberculosis, but to learn more about what it means to be a good human being and live our values.

Paul Farmer lived a life of service to the world. He alleviated the suffering of millions in poverty while coming from a lower-class American background himself. He pulled countless strings, stretched his schedule beyond thin for countless decades, because, in his words, “If I don;t work this hard, someone will die who doesn’t have to” (Kidder 191).

When he had gone to the home of his AIDS patient at the Brigham hospital and learned he was going to get evicted, Farmer signed his check over to him. He only told his wealthy friend, Tom White, when he remarked he looked hungry and asked if he needed any money. When Tom said he made an impractical choice, he said, “Well, God sent you today.”

Farmer was Roman Catholic, and one that was greatly influenced by the ideas of liberation theology in his work. Liberation theology emphasized a preferential option for the poor, an idea that was commonly circulated in PIH, to the point where it had a nickname (O for the P). Preferential option for the poor was a principle that mandated that the needs of the most vulnerable in society should be prioritized. These ideas mirror Matthew 25 of the Bible, which Dr. Farmer uses throughout the book to support his reasoning and decision-making, which often was misunderstood and went against the grain. He was a bright physician who could have easily spent his life in the suburbs of Boston, MA as an esteemed Harvard doctor and physician full-time instead of part-time. He didn’t have to kneel on the dirt-floor of a patient’s hut after a seven hour journey to follow-up on his tuberculosis. He chose this life. And that is what is so admirable and yet sad. The sad part being that people like this in our society are so rare that we see them as a novelty and elevate them to God-like status, something those that knew Paul struggled with as it made them feel as though they could never live up to that standard.

What I admired about Partner in Health’s approach, is that as the nonprofit expanded, they began addressing more and more social determinants of health. For example, after visiting the patient’s hut in Haiti mentioned earlier, he decided the family would get a new house with a concrete floor and metal roof, further arrangements for improving the family’s nutrition, and school tuition for the kids (Kidder 293). PIH also has built several schools in Haiti and, as policy, does not take payment from any impoverished patients. They have also since established several locations in Haiti so patients do not have to make such extensive journeys to seek medical care.

Today, Partners in Health operates in 11 countries: Haiti, Kazakhstan, Lesotho, Liberia, Malawi, Mexico, Navajo Nation, Peru, Rwanda, and Sierra Leone. The vast majority of the staff they employ are from the countries they serve, directly uplifting people and nations and poverty. They also partner with federal governments in all these nations to strengthen public health systems to meet communities and people where they are, and to create sustainable long-term change.

Please consider donating to Partners in Health to support their mission, in Paul Farmer’s words: “Equity is the only acceptable goal” (Kidder 261).

Paul Farmer passed away in 2022 of a heart attack in his sleep while in his Rwanda hospital, but the work he dedicated his life to lives on, and it is up to those of us still alive to fight the good fight.

PATRIAS ES HUMANIDAD.
The only real nation is humanity.
