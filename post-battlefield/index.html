Beyond the Battlefield

The Toll of PTSD and the Fight to Improve Veteran Mental Health

History & Policy · November 7, 2026 · 5 min read

Published in 2011.

“‘They thought she was a terrorist but it was a little girl bouncing a ball in the dark.’ Although he had nothing to do with the event, Carlos visited the girl repeatedly in the hospital, hiding behind a curtain so she wouldn’t know he was there. After he returned home, he began seeing visions in which she was ‘injured or dead or staring at me or pointing at me.’”

— Erin P. Finley, Fields of Combat (58)

Those that serve in the U.S. military face unspeakable horrors and injuries that they often cannot leave at the war zone. Instead, veterans may be negatively affected by their time in service for decades to come.

According to a 2022 cross-sectional study, one of the most common mental health conditions veterans are afflicted by is PTSD (Tedla et al.). Additionally, the Department of Veterans Affairs’ National Center for PTSD reports the following, showing that PTSD occurrence in veterans differs widely by their service era (“How Common”):

Service EraPTSD in the Past YearPTSD at Some Point in Life
Operations Iraqi Freedom (OIF) and Enduring Freedom (OEF) 15 out of 100 (15%) 29 out of 100 (29%)
Persian Gulf War (Desert Storm) 14 out of 100 (14%) 21 out of 100 (21%)
Vietnam War 5 out of 100 (5%) 10 out of 100 (10%)
World War II (WWII) and Korean War 2 out of 100 (2%) 3 out of 100 (3%)

NOTE: The data in this table is from Veterans alive at the time of the study. As such, it does not include Veterans in any service area who have died and may have had PTSD.

Having PTSD can greatly affect important areas of one’s life such as relationships with family, friends, romantic partners, work, and sleep.

For instance, several studies emerged after the Vietnam War showing that families of veterans were hit harder by divorce, substance abuse, and family violence than civilian families (Finley 70). Moreover, spouses of veterans of the Iraq and Afghanistan wars (OIF/OEF) that had PTSD reported more emotional distress, somatic and sleep disorders, less social support, and less marital satisfaction (Finley 70).

Additionally, although rates of partner violence are comparable between active-duty military personnel and the civilian population, rates are 2-3x higher than the national average among veterans with PTSD (Finley 80). In fact, the National Vietnam Veterans Readjustment Study found that 33% of veterans with PTSD reported having been violent with a partner or spouse in the last year– a stark contrast with the 13.5% that was reported among veterans without PTSD (Finley 80). In addition to this, PTSD has also been found to predict violent behavior among WWII veterans that were prisoners of war, and more recent veterans with other severe mental illnesses (Finley 80).

Another area of life in which PTSD can affect veterans negatively is work. For example, a study in San Antonio, TX reported that half of the veterans surveyed reported being unemployed for at least three months since separating from the service (Finley 65). In the same study by Dr. Finley, ~74% of the sample reported having difficulty concentrating for more than ten minutes at a time– a struggle that can prove challenging in school or work. Due to this, ⅓ of the sample had lost at least one job since separating from the military (Finley 65).

Moreover, a recent VA study had shocking results: OEF/OIF veterans with PTSD were 4x more likely to describe thoughts of suicide than were those with no PTSD (Finley 73). Rates were even higher for those with PTSD and another mental illness such as depression or alcohol or drug use (Finley 73).

In addition to this, Latino veterans have significantly greater rates of PTSD even when controlling for strong risk predictors such as cumulative exposure to life stressors (Finley 83). For instance, one study among Vietnam veterans showed that the rate of PTSD was only 13.7% among white veterans in comparison to the 27.9% that was faced by Latinos (Finley 83). This is especially interesting considering that research shows that Latinos generally have better mental health than their non-Latino white counterparts (Finley 83).

One of the greatest obstacles servicemen and veterans face in seeking and receiving mental health services is stigma.

A 2003 report released by the Army’s Mental Health Advisory Team (MHAT) revealed that 59% of soldiers surveyed worried that seeking mental health care would result in their being seen as weak, 49% expressed concern that their unit would have less confidence in them, and 46% feared their leaders would blame them for having a problem (“Operation;” Finley 103). Mental health providers also expressed frustration at the lack of antidepressants and other pharmaceutical medications they could prescribe and the inconsistent accessible care to units posted in remote areas of Iraq (Finley 103).

Furthermore, the Army’s extension of deployments from 12 to 15 months in April 2007 caused concern over how this would affect servicemen and their families long-term (Finley 110; Tyson and White). The tour extension affected active-duty soldiers that were currently deployed or going to Iraq and Afghanistan and was seen as a “difficult but necessary” step by President George W. Bush (Tyson and White).

In 2008, the VA rolled out the use of prolonged exposure therapy (PE), a therapy that was shown to prove significant improvements in many veterans struggling with PTSD (Finley 124). The VA’s standardized rollout of PE that included nationwide workshops teaching VA providers how to utilize these strategies in their work with PTSD paid off in expanding the types of therapies mental health providers had in their toolkit, especially for helping those that psychodynamic therapy was not showing benefits (Finley 124). Integrating exposure therapy into the VA’s standard of care for PTSD has helped transform the mental health condition from how it used to be seen as: “A lifelong disorder, to be managed rather than cured” (Finley 121).

Although less than 10% of OEF/OIF veterans with a new PTSD diagnosis completed the recommended number of treatment sessions within the first year following their diagnosis, it is still the Department of Veterans Affairs’ job to provide high-quality healthcare to those that served our country and the department is accomplishing this mission excellently as of 2026. This year’s Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services annual report showed record-high quality ratings, outperforming non-VA facilities as VA hospitals earned four or five stars 78% of the time (Lawrence). This is a significant increase from 2024 when the Overall Hospital Quality Star Rating was only 58% (Lawrence).
